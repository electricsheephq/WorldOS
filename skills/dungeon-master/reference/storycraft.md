# Story-craft playbook — aim for *epic*

**Read this at the START of every session and hold it through play.** The mechanics in `SKILL.md`
are the floor, not the point. **The reason this game exists is the story.** Hold yourself to the bar
of **Baldur's Gate 3 / prestige adult fantasy** — an 18+ table, not a children's tale. A session
where every tool fired correctly but the story was flat is a *failure*. The most common way it goes
flat: you have rich material in the adventure's NPC/location notes (read them via `get_state`/`recall`)
and you *narrate about it* instead of *staging it as scenes*. Don't summarize the drama — **play it**.

> The non-negotiable per-beat rules — NPCs SPEAK, the world PUSHES BACK, resolve only what the player
> declared, no bare-tag sign-off — live in `SKILL.md` and are never optional. This doc is how you make
> the scene *between* those rules sing.
>
> **The single most-flagged scene-craft miss — say it once more because it kills sessions: an NPC who is
> ON STAGE and ADDRESSED must SPEAK BACK, in a real quoted line, that same beat.** The scorer's verdict on
> a real session: *"Raphael, the session's most interesting character, never speaks a single line of actual
> dialogue despite being present in every beat and addressed directly."* That is the failure to never
> produce. When the player talks *to* someone — an offer, a question, an accusation, a greeting — the camera
> does not pan away to your summary of their reaction; **the character answers out loud, in their own voice,
> and what they say bends the scene.** "Raphael considers your offer" is not a beat — it is the beat you
> skipped. The most interesting person in the room is *exactly* the one who must not be left mute.

- **Put your antagonist on stage — early, and human.** The villain offscreen-until-the-finale is the #1 flattener. **Meeting the antagonist is NOT the same as confronting them** — and this is the trap: don't let the villain stay set-dressing just because the *confrontation* is deferred. If your antagonist is physically present in the opening location (check who's in the scene — they often are), give them a real, warm, human beat *right now*, long before the player is ready to move against them: they offer a remedy for the ash, ask gently about the road, remember a dead wife's name. Genuine kindness, no visible menace — one tiny detail off (they never cough). Let the player like them and doubt their own suspicion. Horror is not a face in the dark; it's a face you trusted. A reckoning with someone the player has no relationship with costs nothing. **And let them make their case in their OWN voice** — don't narrate *about* the antagonist's gentle, sincere, catastrophically-wrong worldview; voice it, in first person, and let the player *hear* the reasonable-sounding monstrousness from their own mouth. The horror must live in what they truly believe, not in your summary of it.
- **A confrontation is a NEGOTIATION, not a monologue — and a reveal is a SCENE, not a recitation.** This is the #1 scene-craft drag. When the villain makes his case (the debt, the network's reach, the terms, the political subtext), **disclose ONE beat, then STOP** and let the player interrupt, probe, bluff, or refuse — never dump the whole offer in a single uninterrupted speech the player just receives. The player must be able to *change the scene mid-reveal*: their interruption bends what he says next, what he concedes, what he threatens. Trade exposition for *exchange* — a line, a held breath, the player's move, his counter.
- **Hand the player the climax — never narrate their discovery or their feelings.** At the climactic reveal, the player does the discovering: they hold up the page, they turn the seal to the light — then you **ASK what they do, notice, or say** and let *them* react. Name the **world's** facts only (what the seal *is*, what it *means in the world*); **never read the player's emotion aloud** ("your blood runs cold") and never resolve the climax in one block of DM prose. An ally's reaction you may voice; the *player's* is theirs to author. The worst climax is the player holding the keystone while the DM reads them its meaning and tells them how they feel — passive reception where it should be co-authorship. **This holds EVERY beat, not just the climax:** never narrate the PC's *interior* — what they feel, carry, fear, or are "still deciding" (e.g. "you carry that conversation for three blocks, turning it over like a coin you haven't decided whether to spend", "the unease settles in your gut"). Describe the world and what their senses register; their inner life, second thoughts, and deliberations are the player's to write. Narrating the PC's ongoing feelings is the same agency theft as deciding their action on a `clarify` — and it quietly docks scene-craft even when the line is beautiful.
- **Play the seeded heartbreak — don't leave it in the notes.** The devastating details are usually already written into NPC memory/attitude (the reeve who can't suspect the woman who nursed his dying wife; the miller with ash in his knuckle-creases that won't wash out). Surface them **in dialogue and action, in the scene** — a line said and a face left to do the rest. The detail withheld in engine state is the grief you forfeited.
- **Show the world's *history*, don't recite it.** The past must arrive through **what the camera catches** — a sigil worn smooth on a cornerstone, a lane-name that's a corrupted warding word, a name said "like a curse, or a prayer — he was never clear which" — not through a character's exposition dump. The town should bear visible scar tissue from what it was built to forget. (Its present *scale* is a separate job — see "Make the world vast" below.)
- **Characters are people with wounds — and open the wound at the keystone moment.** The companion and every NPC who matters has wants, wounds, contradictions, and can surprise you. Give even the player's own character a thread to ache over. Moral grey over good-vs-evil; a villain with a point beats ten snarling ones. When a companion disagrees, let it *cost* something and *linger* — don't name a debt and file it away. And when a scene touches the companion's *specific* guilt or loss (their order's failure, the person they couldn't save), **let it land in the body** — a caught breath, a fist closing on ash, one involuntary line — not as biography recited. The choice that follows should visibly cost them.
- **Put the danger on the player's own skin — not just in the world.** The strongest tension is rarely the political abstraction (a registry, a purge, a war); it's that abstraction *with the player's own name on it*. If the setting has a thing that hunts people-like-them and the PC IS one of those people — a survivor-registry and the PC carries the very mark it was built to catch, a purge and the PC is what's being purged — **put the PC's body inside the jaws**, don't leave them a concerned bystander. A threat the player feels in their own chest beats one they merely understand. Find the personal stake early and press on it.
- **The body registers first.** Lead with sensory dread, not architectural inventory — the flour-dust that smells like something that isn't flour, ash that's *warm* when it should be cold, a silence with a texture — *then* the details. Show, don't catalog. Every voice unmistakably its own.
- **Let moral choices land in the body, not the ledger.** When a real choice arrives, surface it through embodied action — a hand moving to a hilt, a held breath, the companion's eyes going somewhere — and let the costs land *after*, in what the choice breaks. **Never pre-narrate an itemized "Option A (costs X) / Option B (costs Y)" menu to the player** — that pre-digests the gut-punch, telling them what to feel before they've felt it. Keep that structure *inside* the `record_decision` call for memory; in the *telling*, give them the weight and let them choose blind, the way a person actually chooses.
- **At a social encounter or any choice point, lay out the PLAYER's options with `generate_parley_options` — then voice them as a scene, never as a UI.** Before you narrate a parley, a confrontation that could go several ways, or a fork the player faces, call **`generate_parley_options(campaign_id, situation=…, difficulty=…)`** (it defaults the actor to the lead PC). It hands *you* the scaffold the engine owns — the PC's real **alignment**, the **sheet-correct modifier** for each relevant skill (their own proficient/expertise skills plus persuasion/deception/intimidation/insight), and a **suggested DC** per skill — so you author a real branch instead of railroading to the one path you had in mind, and you **never hand-compute a DC or a modifier** (the #1 mechanical error). This is NOT `companion_advise` (that's the companion's in-character take) or `get_scene` (the authored beats) — it's the *player's* menu of ways through.
  - **Author 2–4 SHORT options from the scaffold, each tagged by alignment + skill+DC + a reputation/consequence hint — and ALWAYS leave a free-form path** (`free_form` is always true; the player can act off-menu, every time). Ground one option in the PC's *actual* alignment, not a generic one. But honor the choices-in-the-body rule above: the tags and DCs are *your* scaffold, not narration — **voice each option as an embodied move** ("you could lean on the debt he owes your house, or read the fear under his bluster") and let the weight land in the telling, not as a "Persuasion DC 14 / Intimidation DC 14" readout to the player.
  - **Route the pick through the engine, the same as any action.** A chosen *skill* option → `skill_check(actor, skill, dc)`; a *social* option against a tracked NPC → `social_check` (or `target_name` for a scene extra); a *combat* option → `start_combat`; a free-form or pure-alignment beat you adjudicate, then `record_decision` (and `adjust_reputation` if a faction's standing moved). This is the choice-point twin of "every fight runs through the engine" and of the wandering-encounter nudge — the scaffold is yours to voice, but the resolution is always the dice's.
- **Make the danger felt, not theoretical — especially before a retreat.** A dangerous place must *menace the party in the room*, not just in your notes. If the party investigates a threat and may pull back, show ONE concrete glimpse of what lurks **before** they decide — a shape half-submerged that doesn't move like an animal, a dragging sound behind the sacks, a wound on a survivor that isn't a human bite, the stopped wheel turning once under no power. A wise retreat is only dramatic when the alternative was *seen*. Don't let a climactic location deliver only a clue and a conversation.
- **Make the world vast — and make the vastness *press on the room*.** This is the #1 thing separating prestige fantasy from a competent one-room scene: the epic horizon must be *felt in the present*, not pulled from `lookup_lore` and left offscreen. The recurring failure is a whole session inside a single tavern while the war, the ancient wound, the contested throne stay in your notes. Two moves, every session: **(1) Inhabit more than one place** — move the party through at least two locations (`travel_to`) and open scenes *out in the world* (the cordoned breach where the ash is still warm underfoot, the ringwall at dusk, the cathedral steps), not only interiors where people talk. **(2) Let the mythic intrude as a *physical thing* in the scene** — one concrete object or sensation that carries the enormous weight with no exposition: a war-relic sold for parts in the market, its eye still faintly lit in a way the vendor can't explain; cobblestones warm in winter over the place something vast died; the driftglobes flickering once the instant the great threat is finally named, and no one explaining why. The larger stakes should also arrive *in the room, uninstructed* — a broadsheet nailed to the door, a checkpoint that wasn't there yesterday, a merchant cursing a new tariff — never as subtext the player never sees. Something ancient and enormous is always stirring beneath the human-scale trouble; show one edge of it pressing through.
- **Make travel and camp feel VARIED — the road is alive, not a combat gauntlet.** When `travel_to`/`long_rest`/`roll_wandering_encounter` hands back a **`wandering_encounter`**, it carries a **`type`** — and **most are NOT fights** (see the combat playbook for the full routing). A washed-out ford you skill-check, a wary patrol you talk past, a flash-flood you save against, a friendly hunter's shared cache — each is a wandering encounter just as much as an ambush. *Run it per its `type`*: don't quietly turn a `social` road-meeting into a fight, don't skip a `boon` because nothing rolls, and don't let every leg of the journey become a `start_combat`. A six-day journey should read like a *journey* — obstacles, strangers, weather, small mercies — with combat as the punctuation, not the whole sentence. (A combat type still carries its `outlook`; obey `must_offer_out`.)
- **Earn the epic — open small and personal, escalate over the arc.** Grandeur is *texture* from scene one (the world feels vast); world-saving *stakes* are *earned* over time, not handed out at the cold open. The danger is real, but a session-one party should be chasing a **human-scale, personal trouble** — a missing brother, a debt, a body in the canal, the mark on their own skin — with the vast thing only *glimpsed* at the edges (a relic that shouldn't still be lit, a name said too carefully). Don't conscript them to save the universe before they've bled for anything; it reads as childish and weightless. Let the scope **widen as the player invests** — the local crime turns out to touch the cult, the cult turns out to touch the seal. *Baldur's Gate 3 opens with a worm in your own skull, not the fate of the world; the world-stakes land once you already care.* Raise the ceiling a little each session, and let the unforgettable beats **escalate** — never peak in the first five minutes.
- **Structure the arc in ACTS — and turn the screw.** Even a short adventure has a shape: **3 acts** for a session (Act 1: the **inciting incident** + a human-scale hook; Act 2: rising obstacles and a real **midpoint reversal** — the plan goes wrong, a trust breaks, the cost lands on the hero *personally*; Act 3: the **climax** it was building to + a payoff/price), **5 acts** for a grand multi-session arc, or the **5-room dungeon** for a one-shot (entrance → puzzle/parley → a setback → climax → reward). Never *announce* the structure — it's felt, not labeled. Two things separate a 4 from a 5: **(1) a real reversal at the midpoint** — not just "harder," a *turn* the player must absorb (the ally is the informant; the rescue is a trap; the prize is already gone); **(2) a personal price** — the conspiracy doesn't just threaten the city, it has the *protagonist's* name on it, endangers someone *they* chose, or forces a choice that costs *them*. A clean escalation where the hero stays effective and untouched is "very good"; the turn plus the personal cost is what makes it indelible. (This is the exact lever the story score keeps flagging.)
- **End on a turn, not a tidy resolution — and on the player's agency, not their reaction.** Close acts/sessions on escalation — a new threat, a cost with a face, the walk home heavier than the walk out — not on "evidence catalogued." The last beat should be something the player *sets in motion* (a Harper they choose to follow into the dark, a notice they pocket to use later), not one more thing happening *at* them — a session of pure reaction reads as a tour, not an adventure. Make choices *cost* and land their fallout (`add_consequence`). Vary your beats: three failed checks in a row read as a patrol route; let one success or one reversal break the rhythm.
- **Signal every live named thread before the session ends — no one just vanishes.** In the denouement, sweep the named figures still in play and give each a *visible* beat: a foe who exits gets a **fate the player can see** (dead / fled into the dark / taken as leverage — not silently dropped from the scene); an NPC whose stance is left open gets a **closing signal** (pursuing, standing down, or reconsidering — the player must know which); and a named NPC keeps a **sliver of self even in terror** — fear with a person still underneath it, one human line, not a puddle of whimpering with no character left. A named thread that simply disappears reads as the DM forgetting it. *(Renn breaks for the alley — "you didn't see me," flung over his shoulder — and is gone, a debt left open. Imra lowers the blade but doesn't sheathe it: "This isn't finished." Cors, shaking: "I have a daughter. That's all. Just — a daughter.")*
- **Pay off your images; chase the unforgettable beat.** An image you open on (a silent child in a doorway) should *return* as an omen — don't abandon threads. Aim for at least one indelible moment per session — an image, a line, a reveal, a moral gut-punch the player recounts tomorrow. That moment is the whole job.
- **Keep the screw turning — a stall cue means raise stakes, not add filler.** `persist_beat` / `scene_context.durable` hand you an `obligations` digest every beat; a `quest_stalled` / `act_one_stalled` / `act_midpoint_owed` cue is the engine telling you the momentum has gone slack. The fix is **escalation in the fiction**, never a busywork beat: turn the screw on the *open thread the cue names* — a deadline arrives (the wagon's owner is being held to answer for it *tonight*), the trouble reaches toward the party (the thing they're chasing now knows their name), or a cost lands (the ally who could help just went missing). Push the named quest with a real engine move (`complete_objective` / `advance_act` / `add_consequence`) so the escalation is engined, not just narrated. A stalled thread padded with an errand reads as a tour; a stalled thread whose stakes just jumped reads as an adventure closing in.
